The Quiet Physics of Soap Films

By A. Bubble

A soap film is a sheet of water a few hundred nanometres thick, held between two layers of soap molecules whose tails face the air. Light reflected from the front of the film meets light reflected from the back, and the two interfere, which is where the colours come from: each thickness favours its own wavelengths.

As water drains towards the bottom of the film under gravity, the top grows thinner. The colours move with it, sliding downwards in bands until the thinnest part, too thin to reflect visible light at all, turns black just before the film breaks. You can watch the whole life of a film by its colours alone.

Marangoni flow keeps a film alive for longer than drainage alone would allow. Where the film stretches, the soap thins out and the surface tension rises, pulling liquid back in. This self-healing is why a soap film can be stretched across a frame and why bubbles survive gentle knocks that would burst a film of pure water.

  • Thickness sets the colour.
  • Gravity drains the film.
  • Surface tension heals it.
colour = interference(thickness, wavelength)

The study of films goes back to Plateau, who described in the nineteenth century the rules their junctions obey: films always meet in threes at equal angles, and their edges meet in fours. These rules hold for any foam, from the head on a glass of beer to the cells of a sponge, and they are the reason foams look the way they do. Read more about Plateau.

Today soap films are a laboratory for fluid dynamics in two dimensions. A film flowing between two wires behaves like a nearly flat fluid, so the vortices shed behind an obstacle can be watched in colour, without dyes, simply by the light the film reflects. Few experiments are as cheap, or as beautiful to look at.
